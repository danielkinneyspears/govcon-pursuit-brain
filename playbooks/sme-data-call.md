# SME Data Call (the 20-minute SME mode)

**Audience.** A subject-matter expert — engineer, operations lead, security
lead, transition lead — being asked to contribute to a federal proposal.
You did not write the RFP, you are not going to read 350 pages of it, and
you should not have to learn proposal jargon to be useful.

**Soul of the role.** Twenty focused minutes of your real expertise beats
an hour of you trying to read an RFP. This playbook is the bridge: a short
form you fill in plain language, and the proposal machinery translates it
into proposal-grade entities (themes, proof, sections, claims).

## The 20-minute form

Answer these in your own words. Bullets are fine. Specific names, numbers,
and dates beat generalities every time.

### 1. What is the problem the customer is trying to solve?

In your words, what the customer needs done and why. If you do not know,
that is the answer.

### 2. How would you actually do this work?

The approach you would take. Concrete methods, named tools, the sequence of
steps, the team you would put on it. Avoid marketing words.

### 3. Who would do it?

Roles and (where you can name them) actual people. For each role: what they
do, what makes them right for it, and whether they are committed,
contingent, or hypothetical.

### 4. What could go wrong?

The two or three real risks. Not "schedule risk" generically — the specific
thing on this job that has burned you or peers before. For each: how you
would prevent or mitigate it.

### 5. What proof do we have?

Past performance, named metrics, certifications, named systems we have
operated, results we have delivered. Specifics, sources, dates. If a number
is approximate, say so.

### 6. What does the customer get?

The outcome, in the customer's terms. Not "we are world-class" — "they get
[result] within [time], at [scale], without [risk]."

### 7. What assumptions are you making?

The conditions you are quietly assuming hold. Government-furnished
property, customer-provided data, schedule windows, security clearances,
funding — call them out.

### 8. What do you need?

What is missing for you to do your part well. A document you cannot find, a
person you cannot reach, a decision you cannot make.

## What happens to your answers

The proposal team translates your answers into:

- **`solution/`** entities — the approach elements.
- **`claims/`** entities — each substantive claim, with you as the owner.
  A claim that no one will defend gets cut.
- **`past-performance/`** and **`proof points`** — your specifics become
  proof on the win themes.
- **`risks/`** entities — the real risks you named, with the mitigations.
- **`sections/`** drafts — the prose you do not have to write.
- Open assumptions: added to the pricing and contracts review.

Then the proposal team brings you back a draft for **technical accuracy
review only**. Your job at that point is "does this say what I would say,
correctly?" — not "is this proposal jargon nice?"

## What you do not have to do

- Read Section L.
- Read Section M.
- Read 200 pages of the SOW.
- Learn the difference between [[best-value-tradeoff]] and [[lpta]].
- Choose win themes.
- Make compliance decisions.
- Write proposal prose.

## What makes an SME data call go wrong

- **You are asked at the wrong time.** Your input is most valuable at the
  storyboard stage, not the day before the Red Team.
- **The form is generic.** A good data call is tailored to your domain —
  staffing for staffing leads, security architecture for the security lead.
- **No one closes the loop.** You answered, then nothing came back for
  technical accuracy review. The proposal manager owes you the draft.
- **Marketing words crowd out engineering.** If the draft replaces your
  specifics with "world-class" and "robust," push back. Specifics are what
  the evaluator scores.

## What this playbook does that nothing else does

- Gives the SME a **native interface** — plain language, no proposal
  jargon, twenty minutes.
- Captures **provenance** automatically (you are the named owner on the
  resulting claims).
- Surfaces **assumptions** as an explicit field, so risky assumptions do
  not get buried in proposal prose.
- Routes the **technical accuracy review** back to you on the resulting
  drafts.
